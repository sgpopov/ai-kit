# Why you lost a prompt

Read this before writing recommendations. A diagnosis that names the wrong
cause sends the user to spend months on the wrong work.

For every lost high-intent prompt you have concrete evidence in the run: which
competitors were recommended, how they were described, which pages were cited,
and what the model said about the brand elsewhere in the set. Use it. Generic
advice ("publish more content", "improve your SEO") is a failure of this step.

## The three root causes

Almost every loss is one of these. Pick **one** primary cause per prompt —
naming all three is the same as naming none.

### Coverage

**The competitor is discussed in more places than you are.**

They have the comparison article, the review on a third-party site, the podcast
appearance, the forum thread, the listicle entry. You have a landing page. The
model assembles its answer from what the web says about a brand, and there is
simply more said about them.

*Evidence it is this one:*
- Cited URLs are dominated by third-party domains, not the competitors' own
  sites.
- The competitor appears across many prompts in the set, not just this one.
- The brand appears where the query is narrow and disappears where it is broad
  — a sign of thin overall presence rather than a positioning mismatch.

*What follows:* the work is off-site — getting written about, listed, reviewed,
included in roundups. Around 85% of AI brand mentions trace to third-party
sources, so this is the most common cause and the one most people misdiagnose
as an on-site problem.

### Consistency

**The sources disagree about what you are, so the model stays vague or wrong.**

The site says "for small business owners". LinkedIn says "built for solo
founders". Reviews talk about a different use case entirely. The model cannot
build a confident picture, so it either hedges or omits the brand in favour of
one it can describe cleanly.

*Evidence it is this one:*
- The brand *is* mentioned in other prompts but described differently each time.
- Descriptions are vague, hedged, or contradict `positioning`.
- Sentiment is neutral almost everywhere — the model has nothing sharp to say.

*What follows:* the work is alignment, not volume. One positioning sentence,
then propagate it: homepage, about page, product pages, social bios, directory
listings, review-site profiles, press boilerplate. Publishing more before
fixing this makes the noise louder.

### Specificity

**They own a narrow segment; you address everyone.**

The prompt described a situation. The competitor has a page for exactly that
situation. You have a page about the category. Models prefer the specific
answer because it is more useful.

*Evidence it is this one:*
- The competitor's cited page title matches the prompt almost word for word.
- The brand wins broad prompts in the set and loses narrow ones.
- The competitor is smaller or less known but keeps beating the brand on
  narrow queries.

*What follows:* build the specific page. One page per real scenario:
*"[category] for [audience] with [constraint]"*. This is the fastest of the
three to fix and produces the clearest wins.

### Other

Use sparingly, and say what it actually is: the brand is genuinely not a fit
for the prompt, the market is regional and the brand is not, the category
mapping is wrong, or the model is working from stale information about a recent
rebrand or launch.

## Writing the recommendation

For each lost high-intent prompt produce:

- **Root cause** — one of the four, with the specific evidence from the run.
- **Three actions**, ordered by effort-to-impact. Each must be concrete enough
  to start today: a page to write with a working title, a specific site to get
  listed on, an exact sentence to standardise across profiles.

Prefer naming the artifact over describing the activity. "Write
*'[Product] for [segment] with [constraint]'* and link it from the pricing
page" is actionable. "Improve topical authority" is not.

## Reading descriptions, not just mentions

The richest finding in most audits is not a missing mention — it is a present
one that is wrong. When the brand appears, compare the model's sentence to
`positioning` and check:

- **Does it match the actual position?** Premium described as budget is a
  perception problem worth more than several missing mentions.
- **Is it specific or generic?** "A good option for X" gives a buyer no reason
  to choose. "24/7 phone and chat support including weekends, WordPress-focused"
  does.
- **Does it name the differentiator?** If the model does not understand what
  makes the brand different, neither will the buyer reading the answer.
- **Is it weaker than the competitors' descriptions in the same answer?** If
  theirs are sharper, the model understands their positioning better than
  yours. That is fixable, but only once you have seen it.
